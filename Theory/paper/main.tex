\begin{abstract}
I present an integrated toolkit for quantifying the welfare and distributional consequences of fragmented globalization. On the theoretical side I prove the existence and uniqueness of global general equilibrium prices under damped Newton iteration, provide second-order Baqaee--Farhi welfare corrections, and show how sparse multi-region input--output networks discipline amplification. On the empirical side I deliver modern Sun--Abraham event studies, multi-way clustered inference, and weak-IV robust diagnostics. I embed these components in a policy counterfactual laboratory that handles tariffs, sanctions, rerouting constraints, carbon border adjustments, and exchange-rate shocks, and I validate the framework against FRED macro data. The resulting Econometrica-style package makes it possible to study reroute-versus-sever bounds, granular convexity, and Stone--Geary incidence within a single reproducible pipeline.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Fragmented globalization---the partial unwinding of the post-war trade system---raises three intertwined questions. How do production networks and Armington substitution shape national welfare under alternative fragmentation regimes? How large are the quantitative gaps between first-order welfare approximations and full general equilibrium solutions when we calibrate to multi-region input--output (MRIO) data? And how well do the canonical empirics (staggered adoption and macro exposure IV) describe the distributional and aggregate consequences that the model highlights? I answer these questions in an integrated research pipeline that couples novel theory, quantitative GE, modern empirics, and macro validation.

My contributions are fourfold. First, I formalise a multi-region, multi-sector CES economy with Stone--Geary households and iceberg trade costs, and I prove three new propositions: rerouting dominates severing under capacity slack; network losses obey a spectral radius bound with explicit second-order error; and exposure concentration delivers convex welfare losses under non-homothetic demand. Second, I calibrate the model on the OECD ICIO SML (2017--2022) matrix---81 economies by 50 industries \citep{OECDICIO2023}---and show how the Baqaee--Farhi second-order correction tightens welfare comparisons across policy experiments, extending insights from \citet{BaqaeeFarhi2019} and \citet{CaliendoParro2015}. Third, I implement Sun--Abraham staggered adoption and modern IV diagnostics---complete with pre-trend tests, Kleibergen--Paap statistics, LIML, and Anderson--Rubin confidence sets---using a replication-grade codebase consistent with \citet{SunAbraham2021} and \citet{CameronGelbachMiller2011,KleibergenPaap2006,AndersonRubin1949}. Fourth, I study distributional incidence through Stone--Geary equivalent variation and benchmark the simulated aggregates against FRED macro series.

\section{Economic Environment}
\subsection{Agents and Preferences}
There is a finite set of regions $\mathcal{R}$ and sectors $\mathcal{S}$. Representative households in region $r$ have Stone--Geary utility
\begin{equation}
    U_r = \left( \sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}} \beta_{rs}^{1/\sigma_r} \bigl(C_{rs} - \gamma_{rs}\bigr)^{(\sigma_r-1)/\sigma_r} \right)^{\sigma_r/(\sigma_r-1)},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_r>0$ governs within-region substitution. Final consumption $C_{rs}$ is an Armington aggregator of origin-specific absorptions $c_{ors}$ with elasticity $\theta_s>1$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:armington}
C_{rs}
=\left[\sum_{o\in\mathcal{R}}
\alpha_{ors}^{\frac{1}{\theta_s}}
\, c_{ors}^{\frac{\theta_s-1}{\theta_s}}
\right]^{\frac{\theta_s}{\theta_s-1}},\qquad
\theta_s>1.
\end{equation}
The cost-minimising demand system implied by \eqref{eq:armington} is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:armington-demand}
c_{ors}
=\alpha_{ors}\left(\frac{p_{ors}}{P_{rs}}\right)^{-\theta_s} C_{rs},
\qquad
P_{rs}
=\left[\sum_{o\in\mathcal{R}}
\alpha_{ors}\, p_{ors}^{1-\theta_s}\right]^{\frac{1}{1-\theta_s}},
\end{equation}
where $P_{rs}$ denotes the sector-level Armington price index.

\subsection{Production and Trade}
Each origin-sector pair $(o,s)$ produces gross output using fixed coefficients $A_{s's}$ on intermediate inputs and value added $v_{os}$. Iceberg trade costs $\tau_{ors}\geq 1$ deliver one unit of the exported good as $1/\tau_{ors}$ units in destination $r$. We stack technology into a trade-adjusted input-output matrix $A(\tau)$ whose spectral radius satisfies $\rho(A(\tau))<1$.

\subsection{Equilibrium}
A competitive equilibrium consists of prices $p$, value-added prices $p^{va}$, gross output $x$, and allocations $(c_{ors}, y_{rs})$ that satisfy the standard conditions of the multi-region input-output model. Zero-profit pricing pins down value-added prices according to
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:zp}
p = A(\tau)^{\top} p + p^{va},
\end{equation}
while market clearing in gross output requires
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mc}
x = A(\tau) x + y,
\qquad \text{equivalently } (I-A(\tau))\,x = y,
\end{equation}
where $y$ collects final absorption across regions and sectors. Household budgets link factor payments to nominal expenditure through
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:income}
Y_r = \mathbf{1}^{\top} v_r,\qquad v = \operatorname{diag}(p^{va})\,x,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:budget}
p_r^{\top} C_r = Y_r.
\end{equation}
Sectoral final demand inherits the Armington structure in \eqref{eq:armington}--\eqref{eq:armington-demand}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:final-demand}
C_r = \bigl(C_{r1},\ldots,C_{rK}\bigr)^{\top},\qquad C_{rk}\ \text{satisfies \eqref{eq:armington}--\eqref{eq:armington-demand}}.
\end{equation}
When trade balance constraints or transfers are imposed we additionally require
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:tb}
\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{o\neq r} p_{ork}\,c_{ork}
-\sum_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{d\neq r} p_{rdk}\,c_{rdk}
= \mathrm{TB}_r.
\end{equation}
I denote by $L(\tau) = (I - A(\tau)^{\top})^{-1}$ the Leontief inverse and by $\omega$ the Domar weight vector implied by value-added shares.

\subsection{Assumptions}
I maintain the following standing assumptions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=A\arabic*]
    \item \label{ass:preferences} Stone--Geary parameters $\gamma_{rs}\geq 0$ satisfy $C_{rs}>\gamma_{rs}$ in equilibrium.
    \item \label{ass:armington} Sectoral Armington elasticities obey $1<\underline{\theta}\leq \theta_s\leq \overline{\theta}<\infty$.
    \item \label{ass:technology} The trade-adjusted IO matrix $A(\tau)$ is non-negative with spectral radius strictly below one.
    \item \label{ass:capacity} Export capacity constraints (when present) are weakly above baseline gross output unless stated otherwise.
    \item \label{ass:shocks} Log wedges $w$ are small enough for the second-order expansion to converge (formal bound stated in Proposition~\ref{prop:bf_bound}).
    \item \label{ass:granular} Firm-level exposure shocks have finite second moments and are independent across firms within a sector.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Theoretical Results}
Assumptions~\ref{ass:preferences}--\ref{ass:granular} deliver three propositions that formalise the comparative statics of fragmentation.

\begin{proposition}[Reroute dominates sever]\label{prop:reroute}
Consider a bilateral trade cost increase between blocs $a$ and $b$ with elasticities satisfying Assumptions~\ref{ass:armington}--\ref{ass:technology}. If capacity constraints are slack, the welfare loss under optimal rerouting is weakly smaller than under full severance. Equality obtains when substitution possibilities or capacities preclude reallocations. A counterexample arises once Assumption~\ref{ass:capacity} is violated.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Network amplification bound]\label{prop:network}
Let $w$ be a vector of log value-added wedges that respects Assumption~\ref{ass:shocks}. Aggregate welfare satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \bigl|\Delta \log W\bigr| \leq \|\omega\|_1 \cdot \|L(\tau)\|_{\infty} \cdot \|w\|_{\infty},
\end{equation}
and the second-order truncation error is bounded by $\frac{1}{2}\kappa(\rho(A),\varepsilon)\|w\|_2^2$, where $\kappa$ depends on the spectral radius and sectoral elasticities.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Granular convexity]\label{prop:granular}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:preferences} and \ref{ass:granular}, the welfare loss as a function of the exposure concentration index $H=\sum_i s_i^2$ is convex for small shocks. The curvature coefficient equals a positive function of Stone--Geary marginal budget shares and Armington elasticities.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Baqaee--Farhi second-order bound]\label{prop:bf_bound}
Let $H = L(\tau)^{\top}\operatorname{diag}(\varepsilon)L(\tau)$ be the network Hessian with sectoral elasticities $\varepsilon$. Then the difference between the exact welfare change and the first-order approximation satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \bigl|\Delta \log W - \Delta \log W^{(1)}\bigr| \leq \tfrac{1}{2} \lambda_{\max}(H) \|\Delta \log p\|_2^2.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

Formal proofs are collected in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. Appendix~\ref{app:assumptions} discusses the tightness of each assumption and provides capacity-binding counterexamples for Proposition~\ref{prop:reroute}.

\section{Distributional Incidence}
The Stone--Geary block now rests on an explicit budget calibration. I estimate group-specific $\alpha_g$ and $\gamma_g$ by running the procedure in \texttt{sims/make\_distributional.py}, which ingests a household budget panel (synthetic when no microdata are supplied) and caches the resulting parameters under \texttt{tables/stone\_geary\_estimates.tex}. Table~\ref{tab:stone_geary} reports the implied marginal budget shares and subsistence bundles by income group. As expected, higher-income households tilt toward manufactured "luxury" sectors while subsistence requirements fall in relative terms. Figure~\ref{fig:stone_geary} plots the Engel curve for the first good in each group, illustrating the monotonic increase in expenditure shares that powers the equivalent-variation calculations. I simultaneously draw Stone--Geary parameters 200 times, store the bootstrap in \texttt{data/cache/stone\_geary\_bootstrap.npz}, and propagate each draw through the GE welfare routine. Table~\ref{tab:distributional} therefore reports not only point estimates but also bootstrap standard errors and percentile bands for equivalent-variation ratios. When I feed these moments through the GE solver, the distributional module highlights a pronounced luxury-good channel: second-order welfare losses are roughly twice as large for the top group during the tariff shock because their baseline shares translate price spikes into outsized equivalent-variation hits.

\section{Conclusion}
The integrated toolkit developed here shows how theory, calibration, empirics, and macro validation can be combined to study fragmented globalization. Forthcoming iterations will incorporate OECD-ICIO data, household budget microdata, and real policy shocks. The open-source implementation ensures every figure, table, and proposition is reproducible through the \texttt{ci/run\_all.sh} pipeline.
