## 3. Primary Justification — why Vietnam

I almost defaulted to a big, obvious economy, but the point of this project is to
understand an economy, not just describe a famous one — and Vietnam is where the
big question of development economics, how a poor country becomes a rich one, is
being tested in real time.

1. **A clean natural experiment in export-led growth.** Vietnam grew about 7% in
   2024 while much of the world slowed, powered by a manufacturing boom as firms
   moved supply chains out of China (the "China + 1" strategy) — a clear engine
   to analyze in Part 1.
2. **The data is genuinely good.** GDP, inflation, trade, and sector breakdowns
   are all on the World Bank and Trading Economics with recent years filled in.
3. **The standard-of-living story is vivid.** Vietnam went from one of the
   world's poorest countries in the 1980s to a solid middle-income economy in a
   single generation, which makes the Part 2 work (Big Mac Index, Dollar Street)
   concrete instead of abstract.

Poland and Chile are strong backups, but each is a more "finished" story; Vietnam
is the one where I genuinely don't know how the next chapter ends, which makes it
the most interesting to audit.

---

## 4. Focus Question

> **Can Vietnam avoid the "middle-income trap"? As wages rise and its cheap-labor
> advantage fades, can it move up the value chain — from assembling other
> countries' products to designing and branding its own — fast enough to keep
> growing at 6–7% a year, or will growth stall the way it did for several of its
> Southeast Asian neighbors?**

It's specific, researchable with public data, and connects to every later part:
GDP composition and shock exposure (Part 1), rising wages and living standards
(Part 2), the State Bank of Vietnam's currency and industrial policy (Part 3), and
a "one reform that decides the outcome" macro pitch (Part 4).
