## 3. Primary Justification — why Vietnam

I almost defaulted to a big, obvious economy, but the whole point of this
project is to *understand* an economy, not just describe a famous one — and
Vietnam is the rare country where the central drama of development economics is
playing out right now, in fast-forward.

Three reasons it won the top slot:

1. **It's a clean natural experiment in export-led growth.** Vietnam grew ~7% in
   2024 while much of the world slowed, powered by a manufacturing boom as
   companies diversified supply chains out of China (the "China + 1" strategy).
   That gives me a clear, traceable engine to analyze in Part 1.

2. **The data is genuinely good.** GDP, inflation, trade, and sector breakdowns
   are all on the World Bank and Trading Economics with recent years filled in.
   Nothing kills a research project faster than missing data, and Vietnam
   doesn't have that problem.

3. **The standard-of-living story is vivid.** Vietnam went from one of the
   world's poorest countries in the 1980s to a solid middle-income economy in a
   single generation. That makes the Part 2 work (Big Mac Index, Dollar Street)
   concrete instead of abstract — there are real, photographable changes in how
   people live.

Poland and Chile are strong backups, but each is a slightly "finished" story:
Poland's convergence with Western Europe is well underway, and Chile's economy
is growing slowly (~2.6% in 2024) and is dominated by copper. Vietnam is the one
where I genuinely don't know how the next chapter ends — which makes it the most
interesting to audit.

## 4. Focus Question

> **Can Vietnam avoid the "middle-income trap"? As wages rise and its
> cheap-labor advantage fades, can it move up the value chain — from assembling
> other countries' products to designing and branding its own — fast enough to
> keep growing at 6–7% a year? Or will growth stall the way it did for several
> of its Southeast Asian neighbors?**

Why this question is a good fit for the project:

- **Part 1 (GDP composition & shock):** I can look at whether growth is shifting
  from low-value assembly toward higher-value industry and services, and stress
  test how exposed Vietnam is to a trade shock.
- **Part 2 (standard of living):** Rising wages are exactly what threatens the
  cheap-labor model — so the citizen's-eye view directly informs the question.
- **Part 3 (policy audit):** The State Bank of Vietnam's handling of the dong and
  the government's industrial policy are the tools that decide the outcome.
- **Part 4 (the macro pitch):** "Here's the one reform that decides whether
  Vietnam breaks the trap" is a natural smoking-gun recommendation to build
  toward.

It's specific, it's researchable with public data, and I honestly don't know the
answer yet — which is the point.
